# Paper trail, step 2 — Research notes with sourced claims

Every factual claim the narration will make is listed here with its source
**before** scripting, so the script writer cannot invent a number. The claim
IDs are carried through the storyboard and the QC report.

| Claim ID | Claim (as it may appear in narration) | Source | Confidence notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| claim-001 | The first published experiments on recall during learning date to 1909. | Abbott, E. E. (1909). On the analysis of the factor of recall in the learning process. *Psychological Monographs*, 11(1), 159–177. | Solid; commonly cited as the earliest study of the recall effect. Narration says "goes back to 1909" — kept vague on purpose. |
| claim-002 | In a 2006 experiment, students who practiced recalling a passage remembered substantially more one week later than students who reread it. | Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention. *Psychological Science*, 17(3), 249–255. | Solid. Narration deliberately says "substantially more" rather than a specific percentage, because the exact gap depends on the condition and retention interval. |
| claim-003 | A 2017 meta-analysis found practice testing helps across ages, subjects, and formats. | Adesope, O. O., Trevisan, D. A., & Sundararajan, N. (2017). Rethinking the use of tests: A meta-analysis of practice testing. *Review of Educational Research*, 87(3), 659–701. | Solid. Narration says "a 2017 review of dozens of experiments" — the meta-analysis aggregates many studies. |
| claim-004 | Difficult but successful retrieval strengthens memory more than easy restudy — a "desirable difficulty". | Bjork, R. A., & Bjork, E. L. (2011). Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way: Creating desirable difficulties to enhance learning. In *Psychology and the Real World* (pp. 56–64). Worth Publishers. | Solid concept; attribution to the Bjorks. |
| claim-005 | Practice testing is one of the techniques rated "high utility" in a major review of learning techniques. | Dunlosky, J., Rawson, K. A., Marsh, E. J., Nathan, M. J., & Willingham, D. T. (2013). Improving students' learning with effective learning techniques. *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 14(1), 4–58. | Solid. Used as backing for segment 5 (methods), not restated numerically. |
| claim-006 | Retrieval practice outperformed elaborative concept mapping in a direct comparison. | Karpicke, J. D., & Blunt, J. R. (2011). Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping. *Science*, 331(6018), 772–775. | Solid, but cut from the script to stay inside segment budgets. Kept in the ledger for the written companion piece. |

## Honesty notes

- No claim says retrieval practice is the *only* thing that works, or that it
  works for everyone; the script stays at the level the sources support.
- effect-size numbers from claim-003 are intentionally **not** quoted in the
  narration (single-study effect sizes vary; a meta-analytic average invites
  misquotation). If a future script quotes them, this ledger must carry the
  exact statistic and page.
- The sample video built from these notes is a **demonstration output** of the
  pipeline. It has not been tested with learners, and no learning-gain claim is
  made anywhere in this repo.
