Full text

Close this book. Try to remember what you just read. Harder than rereading it, right? That struggle is the point. It is called retrieval practice, and it is one of the best-studied ways to make learning stick. Retrieval practice means pulling information out of your memory instead of putting it in again. Rereading feels productive because the page looks familiar. But familiarity is not the same as being able to recall it later, without the book. The evidence goes back to 1909, but a clear modern test came in 2006. Two researchers asked students to either reread a passage or practice recalling it. One week later, the recall group remembered substantially more. A 2017 review of dozens of experiments found the same pattern across ages and subjects. Why does struggling to recall help? Each time you pull a memory out, your brain rebuilds the path to it, and the path gets easier to find next time. Psychologists call this a desirable difficulty. The effort is not a sign of failure, it is part of how the memory gets stronger. You can use this in three ways. Write practice questions and answer them from memory. Make flashcards and grade yourself honestly. Or do a brain dump: close your notes and write down everything you remember about the topic, then check what you missed. So the next time you study, skip the highlighter marathon. Close the book first and try to recall. When recall is hard, that is when learning is happening.
